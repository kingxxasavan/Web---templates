How it works

Vertex drafts

You approve

Edit anything, then publish to a hosted page, your own site via API, email, or a Slack channel.

Grouping

Forty merges, six things worth reading

Most releases are thirty commits of plumbing and four changes anyone outside the team cares about. Vertex sorts one from the other and says so.

  • Related pull requests collapse into a single entry
  • Dependency bumps and refactors are hidden by default
  • Breaking changes are pulled to the top, always
  • Every entry links back to the pull requests behind it

FAQ

The things you're about to ask

Does it publish without me looking at it?

Only if you turn that on. The default is that every entry waits for approval, and most teams keep it that way. Auto-publish exists for teams that trust the draft quality after a month or two.

What does it read from my repository?

Pull request titles, descriptions, labels and the merge commit. Not your source code. The GitHub App requests read access to metadata and pull requests only, and you can see exactly what it asked for on the install screen.

Our pull request titles are terrible. Will this work?

Better than you'd think, because the description and the linked issue usually carry the intent even when the title says "fix stuff". Where there's genuinely nothing to work with, Vertex flags the entry rather than inventing something.

Can I self-host it?

Not today. It's on the roadmap for teams on the Company plan, with no date attached — we'd rather not promise a quarter and miss it.

What happens if I cancel?

Your changelog stays up and your API keeps serving. You drop to the free plan's limits, and the custom domain stops resolving after thirty days. Nothing is deleted.
