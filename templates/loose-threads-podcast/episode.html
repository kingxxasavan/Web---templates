The last bell foundry in town

With Agnes Thorne, bellfounder48 min26 May 2026

Agnes has cast church bells for 31 years. We stand two metres from a pour at 1,100°C and learn why a bell is tuned by taking metal away.

Agnes Thorne has cast bells for churches, schools, ships and one very determined football club for 31 years. Her foundry, on the edge of a market town, is one of only a handful left in Britain that still makes bells the old way: in loam moulds, by hand, one at a time.

We spend a long, hot day with Agnes and her team as they pour a new tenor bell for a village church, and we stay for the bit nobody sees: the tuning, where a bell is made to sing by cutting metal away, a sliver at a time.

Mentioned in this episode

  • The bell foundry’s open days (twice a year, book early)
  • “Change ringing” and why bell ringers count in numbers, not notes
  • The cracked handbell, which now lives on Agnes’s own desk

Sound

[A long, low bell note. It hums for almost twenty seconds before fading.]

Hana

That’s a two-tonne bell called Margaret, and the woman who made her is standing next to me with her fingers in her ears.

Agnes

You never get used to it. You just get better at pretending you have.

Joe

Welcome to Loose Threads, the show about how things are made. I’m Joe Whitfield.

Hana

And I’m Hana Mori. This week we’re at one of the last bell foundries in Britain, and I have never been this hot in my life.

Agnes

My first casting was a handbell. It cracked. My boss put it on his desk and kept it there for twenty years, so I’d remember.

Joe

That’s either very kind or very cruel.

Agnes

Both. That’s foundry work.

Hana

So the mould is made of… what is this?

Agnes

Loam. Sand, clay, horse hair and a bit of manure. The hair holds it together as it dries. Recipes haven’t changed much in five hundred years.

Joe

Okay, they’re about to pour, and everyone has gone very quiet.

Agnes

Bronze at about eleven hundred degrees. Seventy-eight per cent copper, twenty-two tin. If it’s too cool it won’t fill the mould; too hot and it shrinks badly. You get one go.

Hana

And once it’s cool, it still isn’t finished?

Agnes

No, now we tune it. We put it on a lathe and shave metal from the inside. You can’t add metal back, so every cut is a decision.

Joe

Why are there so few foundries left?

Agnes

A church bell lasts five hundred years. It’s the best-made thing you can buy, and that’s terrible for business.

Hana

What would you tell someone who wants to learn this?

Agnes

Be patient, wear good boots, and keep your first cracked bell. You’ll want to see how far you’ve come.
